\title{Fitting Covariance vs Response}
\author{
  Sina Tootoonian, Claude 
}
\maketitle{}
We fit connectivity in two ways: by fitting the raw responses, and fitting the covariance of the responses. How are the resulting connectivities related?

When we fit the responses, our loss is $$ \loss{resp}(\ZZ) = \half \frob{\YY - \ZZ \XX} + \half{\la}\frob{\ZZ - \II},$$ while when fitting covariances, we minimize $$ \loss{cov}(\ZZ) = \half \frob{ \YY^T \JJ \YY - \XX^T \ZZ^T \JJ \ZZ \XX }  + \half{\la} \frob{\ZZ - \II}.$$

To relate these, we use the decomposition into mean-subtracted and mean components.
$$ \XX = \JJ \XX + (\II - \JJ)\XX \triangleq \wt{\XX} + \ol{\XX}.$$

Notice that these two components are orthogonal,
$$ \langle \wt{\XX}, \ol{\XX} \rangle = \tr(\wt{\XX}^T \ol{\XX}) = \tr(\XX^T \JJ^T (\II - \JJ) \XX )=\tr(\XX^T \bzero \XX) = \bzero.$$

That means that we can decompose our response loss into two independent components,\begin{align*} \loss{resp}(\ZZ) = \loss{resp}(\JJ \ZZ, \ol{\ZZ})&= \half \frob{ \JJ \YY  - \JJ \ZZ \XX} + \half{\la}\frob{\JJ \ZZ - \JJ}\\ &+ \half \frob{\ol{\YY} - \ol{\ZZ}\XX} +  \half{\la}\frob{\ol{\ZZ} - \ol{\II}}.
\end{align*}

We can do the same thing for the covariance loss,
\begin{align*}
  \loss{cov}(\ZZ) = \loss{cov}(\JJ \ZZ, \ol{\ZZ}) &= \half\frob{(\JJ \YY)^T \JJ \YY - \XX^T (\JJ \ZZ)^T (\JJ \ZZ) \XX} + \half{\la}\frob{\JJ \ZZ -\JJ} \\ & + \half{\la}\frob{\ol{\ZZ} - \ol{\II}}.
\end{align*}

From this, we immediately see that the regularization in the covariance case will send $\ol{\ZZ}$ to $\ol{\II}$.

Therefore, the aspect of the connectivity to compare between the two losses is $\JJ \ZZ$. We can polar decompose this into the product of a pure rotation $\RR$ and a positive-semi-definite stretch $\SS$, $$ \JJ \ZZ = \RR \SS.$$

Since $(\JJ \ZZ)^T (\JJ \ZZ) = \SS^T \RR^T \RR \SS = \SS^T \SS,$ we can see that the covariance fit only determines the stretch component, while the response fit determines both.

Therefore, when comparing connectivity solutions, we should use $\RR$ and $\SS$ computed from $\JJ \ZZ$, not $\ZZ$, when determine the effects of each component.

\section*{Response Fits}
Returning to the response fits, we find that Free fit, and its symmetrically constrained version, give the same performance. In fact, the symmetrically constrained one might be a little better. What do the fits look like?

The free fits is easy. The gradient is $$ \nabla \loss{resp} = -(\YY - \ZZ \XX)\XX^T + \lambda (\ZZ - \II).$$ Setting this to zero gives
$$ \ZZ = (\YY \XX^T + \lambda \II)  (\XX \XX^T + \lambda \II)^{-1} .$$
If we define $$ \bSig_{xx} \triangleq \XX \XX^T + \lambda \II, \quad \bSig_{xy} \triangleq \XX \YY^T + \lambda \II, \quad \bSig_{yx} \triangleq \YY \XX^T + \lambda \II,$$
we get $$ \ZZ_\text{free} = \bSig_{yx} \bSig_{xx}^{-1}.$$
This also gives $$ \ZZ_\text{free}^T = \bSig_{xx}^{-1} \bSig_{yx}^T = \bSig_{xx}^{-1} \bSig_{xy}.$$

To constrain the solution to be symmetric, we add a Lagrange multiplier to the loss, and get $$ \loss{sym} = \loss{resp} + \tr(\bLa^T (\ZZ - \ZZ^T)).$$

The gradient then becomes
\begin{align*} \nabla \loss{sym} &= -(\YY - \ZZ \XX)\XX^T + \lambda (\ZZ - \II) + \bLa - \bLa^T.\\
  \nabla \loss{sym}^T &= -\XX(\YY^T - \XX^T \ZZ) + \lambda (\ZZ - \II) + \bLa^T - \bLa.
\end{align*}
Adding them when the gradient is zero eliminates the Lagrange multipliers and gives
$$ \XX \YY^T + \YY \XX^T + 2\lambda \II = \ZZ \XX \XX^T + \XX \XX^T \ZZ + 2\lambda \ZZ,$$
or, in terms of our definitions, 
$$ \bSig_{xy} + \bSig_{yx} = \ZZ \bSig_{xx} + \bSig_{xx}\ZZ.$$

$\bSig_{xx}$ is invertible, so we get equivalently $$ \bSig_{xx}^{-1} \bSig_{xy} + \bSig_{xx}^{-1} \bSig_{yx} = \bSig_{xx}^{-1}\ZZ \bSig_{xx} + \ZZ,$$ or equivalently
$$ \bSig_{xx}^{-1} \bSig_{xy}\bSig_{xx}^{-1} + \bSig_{xx}^{-1} \bSig_{yx} \bSig_{xx}^{-1}  = \bSig_{xx}^{-1}\ZZ  + \ZZ  \bSig_{xx}^{-1}.$$ 
Then, using our expressions for the free solution, we get $$ \ZZ_\text{free}^T \bSig_{xx}^{-1} + \bSig_{xx}^{-1} \ZZ_\text{free} = \bSig_{xx}^{-1} \ZZ + \ZZ \bSig_{xx}^{-1},$$
or $$ \bSig_{xx} \ZZ_\text{free}^T \bSig_{xx}^{-1} + \ZZ_\text{free} = \ZZ + \bSig_{xx} \ZZ \bSig_{xx}^{-1}. $$

If we work in the eigenbasis of $\bSig_{xx}$,  using the tilde to indicate the coefficients, we can express those $\wt S_{ij}$ of the symmetric solution in terms of those $\wt F_{ij}$ of the free solution as $$ \wt{S}_{ij} = {\wt F_{ji} D_i + \wt F_{ij} D_j \over D_i + D_j},$$ where $D_i$ are the eigenvalues of $\bSig_{xx}$. 

$$\blacksquare$$

\section*{What the data can see: the uniform direction}

The decomposition above splits $\ZZ$ by what the covariance loss can see. There
is a second, finer split, by what the DATA can see, and it explains why the
symmetric fit behaves differently from the free one along one direction.

Per-odour normalization makes every column of $\XX$ sum to zero over rois,
$\XX^T \bone = \bzero$. Let $\ee = \bone / \sqrt{m}$ be the unit vector along the
uniform direction and let $\BB$ be any $m \times (m-1)$ matrix whose columns are
an orthonormal basis of $\bone^\perp$, so that $\QQ = [\ee \; \BB]$ is
orthogonal. Every $\ZZ$ then has the block form
$$ \QQ^T \ZZ \QQ = \begin{bmatrix} a & \rr^T \\ \cc & \MM \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
a = \ee^T \ZZ \ee, \quad \cc = \BB^T \ZZ \ee, \quad \rr = \BB^T \ZZ^T \ee,
\quad \MM = \BB^T \ZZ \BB. $$
Reading the blocks by what they map to what: $a$ takes uniform input to uniform
output, $\cc$ takes uniform input to patterned output, $\rr$ takes patterned
input to uniform output -- how strongly an input pattern drives the MEAN over
rois -- and $\MM$ takes patterned input to patterned output. Nothing below
depends on which basis $\BB$ is: $a$, $\|\cc\|$, $\|\rr\|$ and the spectrum of
$\MM$ are invariant under $\BB \to \BB \OO$ for orthogonal $\OO$.

Because every column of $\XX$ lies in $\bone^\perp$, the product $\ZZ \XX$
involves only $\ZZ \BB$, which is to say only $\rr$ and $\MM$. So the response
loss determines $\rr$ and $\MM$ and says nothing whatever about $a$ or $\cc$;
the regularizer $\half{\la}\frob{\ZZ - \II}$ sets those, giving $a = 1$ and
$\cc = \bzero$. The covariance loss sees even less: $\JJ$ annihilates the
uniform component of the output, so $\rr$ is invisible to it as well, and its
regularizer sets $\rr = \bzero$ too.

Imposing symmetry couples the two. $\ZZ = \ZZ^T$ forces $\cc = \rr$, so the
data-determined $\rr$ is mirrored into the $\cc$ the data cannot constrain.
This is not a failure of the optimizer: $a$ still comes out at exactly $1$,
which is the one number that is genuinely free. It is the constraint acting on
a block where the data is one-sided.

The consequence is visible in $\ZZ$'s spectrum, since
$$ \ZZ \bone = \sqrt{m}\,(a \ee + \BB \cc) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\|\ZZ \bone - \bone\|^2 = m\left[(a-1)^2 + \|\cc\|^2\right], $$
so a mirrored $\cc$ moves $\ZZ$ away from fixing $\bone$, and the eigenvectors
that carry the uniform direction carry the effect. On the matched rois, the free
response fit has $\|\rr\| = 2.06$ with $\|\cc\| = 0$, the covariance fit has both
zero, and the symmetric refit has $\|\cc\| = \|\rr\| = 1.86$. The symmetric
refit's two extreme eigenvalues, $+2.74$ and $-2.09$, have overlaps of $0.67$ and
$0.50$ with $\ee$; restricted to $\MM$ they are $+2.05$ and $-1.23$.

The moral for the constrained refits -- symmetric, PSD, rotation, orthogonal --
is that a constraint imposed on $\ZZ$ reaches this block, where one side of it
was never measured. A constraint imposed on $\MM$ alone, leaving $a$, $\cc$ and
$\rr$ to the regularizer, tests the same hypothesis about the part of the
connectivity the data actually determines.
